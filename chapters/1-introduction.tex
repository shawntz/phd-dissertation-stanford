\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Episodic memory --- the capacity to mentally revisit specific past experiences --- is one of the most consequential capacities of the human mind. It supports learning from a single exposure, navigation through novel environments, the maintenance of personal identity over time, and the planning of future action. Yet despite its centrality, episodic memory is remarkably variable. The same individual, encountering the same material under nominally similar conditions, may at one moment retrieve vividly and at another fail to access the trace at all. Understanding the sources of this within-person variability --- and, ultimately, harnessing them --- is the broad concern of this dissertation.

\section{Memory Expression Varies in the Moments Before Remembering}

Classical theories of memory have largely centered on what is encoded and how that content is organized in the brain. An increasingly compelling body of work, however, suggests that whether a memory can be expressed at any given moment depends not only on the integrity of the underlying trace but also on the state of the broader cognitive system in the seconds immediately preceding the attempt to remember \parencite{madore2020memory,madore2022readiness}. These moment-to-moment states are themselves shaped by interacting fluctuations in attention, arousal, and goal-directed control --- fluctuations that occur on timescales ranging from hundreds of milliseconds to tens of seconds, and that can be assayed non-invasively through measures such as pupillometry, scalp EEG, and behavioral readouts of sustained attention.

The intuition that attention shapes memory is itself far from new. \textcite{ebbinghaus1885memory}, in inaugurating the experimental study of memory, treated attention as a precondition for learning, and the century of work that followed has repeatedly returned to attention as a modulator of mnemonic outcomes \parencite{baddeley1984attention,craik1996effects}. What is new is our capacity to measure these constructs continuously --- to track, in real time, the rise and fall of arousal and attention as people encode and attempt to retrieve information --- and, increasingly, our capacity to intervene on those states experimentally rather than merely observing them. These developments motivate a shift in how the field approaches attention--memory interactions: from inference based on between-condition averages toward state-resolved, within-trial analyses that more faithfully mirror the temporal grain of cognition itself.

\section{The Readiness-to-Remember Framework}

A productive framing for this new generation of work is the \textit{readiness-to-remember} hypothesis: the idea that successful retrieval depends not only on the quality of the original encoding but also on a preparatory neurocognitive state in the seconds just before the retrieval attempt \parencite{madore2022readiness}. Readiness, on this view, is not a single construct but a dynamic confluence of arousal, sustained attention, goal-state maintenance, and proactive control. When these components align --- when arousal sits near optimum, attention is engaged, and the retrieval goal is well-represented --- remembering succeeds. When they do not, even well-encoded content can fail to surface.

Pupillometry has emerged as a particularly informative window onto this preparatory state. Tonic and phasic pupil dynamics covary with locus coeruleus--norepinephrine (LC--NE) activity \parencite{joshi2016relationships,joshi2020pupil}, track moment-to-moment fluctuations in arousal and effort, and are sensitive to both bottom-up and top-down processes thought to regulate cognitive performance. Importantly, pupil-indexed states have been linked not only to perceptual and attentional outcomes but also to encoding and retrieval success in episodic memory tasks \parencite{madore2020memory,robison2022pupillary,unsworth2017importance}. This makes the pupil a natural readout around which to organize the empirical work that follows in this dissertation.

\section{From Correlation Toward Causation}

Two interrelated challenges stand in the way of leveraging pupil-indexed preparatory state to advance theory and application. The first is methodological: pupillometry preprocessing has historically lacked the kind of standardized, reproducible infrastructure that has matured in adjacent modalities such as fMRI and M/EEG \parencite{esteban2019fmriprep,delorme2004eeglab,gramfort2013meg}. The resulting analytic flexibility --- consequential choices about blink handling, artifact rejection, filtering, baseline correction, and the like --- is a source of unmodeled between-study variance and, more pressingly, of within-study garden-of-forking-paths risk \parencite{gelman2013garden,simmons2011false}. Without a shared, transparent pipeline, even the most carefully designed pupillometry experiments are difficult to compare or to reproduce.

The second challenge is inferential. Even when measurement is well-handled, observed correlations between preparatory pupil dynamics and memory outcomes leave open the question of \textit{causality}. Are suboptimal preparatory states a cause of memory failure, or a correlate of some upstream factor that affects both? And if they are causal, can intervention on those states improve memory expression in real time? Answering these questions requires moving beyond observational designs toward closed-loop paradigms that monitor preparatory state online and intervene on it in the moment.

\section{Dissertation Overview}

This dissertation pursues both challenges, alongside a broader theoretical synthesis, across four substantive chapters that progress from theory to tools to conceptual framework to causal experiment.

Chapter~\ref{ch:attending-to-remember} (\textit{Attending to Remember}) reviews recent advances in how attention shapes learning and remembering. It surveys the frontoparietal networks that support attention and cognitive control, evidence for rhythmic dynamics linking attention and memory, age-related changes in the precision of remembering, and the methodological turn toward closed-loop designs that enable causal inference. The chapter establishes the conceptual scaffolding on which the empirical and methodological work that follows is built.

Chapter~\ref{ch:eyeris} (\textit{\texttt{eyeris}}) addresses the methodological prerequisite for rigorous psychophysiological work in this space. It introduces \texttt{eyeris}, an R package that provides a transparent, modular, and FAIR-aligned preprocessing pipeline for pupillometry data, along with interactive diagnostic reports and a BIDS-like derivative structure. The package is offered as a community resource that aims to bring pupillometry preprocessing into parity with the established conventions of other neuroimaging modalities, both to ease reproducibility within individual studies and to facilitate cumulative progress across them.

Chapter~\ref{ch:biofeedback-framework} (\textit{A Biofeedback Framework for Attention and Memory}) lays out the conceptual design space from which the empirical work was drawn. Developed early in my doctoral training, the chapter sketches a taxonomy of four closed-loop pupillometry implementations differing in temporal grain (trial-by-trial vs.\ millisecond-by-millisecond) and inference strategy (fixed thresholding vs.\ individualized predictive modeling). Although it never matured into a published paper, it is included here because it foreshadows --- and in places explicitly anticipates --- the design choices that defined the eventual PRISM paradigm reported in the chapter that follows.

Chapter~\ref{ch:prism} (\textit{PRISM}) puts both the theoretical framing and the methodological infrastructure to work. Leveraging real-time readouts of tonic pupil diameter as a proxy for moment-to-moment arousal and attention, the PRISM (Pupil-based Real-time Intervention System for Memory) paradigm continuously monitors preparatory state during episodic retrieval and, on a subset of trials, delivers a brief reorienting cue during pupil-indexed suboptimal states. Across 67 participants, this closed-loop intervention causally rescues item memory from preparatory suboptimal states --- though not source memory --- and reveals that the rescue is bounded by both the depth and directionality of suboptimality, in a manner consistent with the classic inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance \parencite{yerkes1908relation,astonjones2005integrative}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} (\textit{Conclusions}) draws these threads together. It synthesizes the contributions of the three preceding chapters, discusses their joint implications for the readiness-to-remember framework and for the broader theory of attention--memory interactions, and articulates open questions and promising directions for future inquiry --- including the translational potential of closed-loop methods for educational, clinical, and aging contexts.

\section{A Unifying Thread}

Taken together, the chapters that follow articulate a vision in which the moment-to-moment dynamics of arousal and attention are not merely modulators of remembering but tractable targets for measurement and intervention. By bridging theory, measurement, and causal experimentation, this dissertation seeks to advance our mechanistic understanding of why we remember when we do --- and to lay the groundwork for technologies that may eventually help us remember better, when it matters most.
